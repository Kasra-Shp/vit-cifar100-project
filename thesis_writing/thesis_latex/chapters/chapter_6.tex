\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{ch:discussion-conclusion}

\section{Main Findings}
\label{sec:ch6-main-findings}

The primary benchmark of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} (CIFAR-100 $5\times20$,
seed 42) places the highest observed SimpleAvg result in this run
(SimpleAvg + KD, 75.21\%) 4.45 percentage points above the highest observed
RankExt result in this run (RankExt + FactorOrth + KD + Protect, 70.76\%).
Two observations shape the rest of this chapter. First, the four SimpleAvg
variants stay close to a strong plain baseline (75.16\%): none of KD,
FactorOrth, or their combination produces a clear all-seen improvement.
Second, the four RankExt variants span a far wider range (34.99\% to
70.76\%), and the largest observed changes are associated with the
KD+Protect-bearing configurations ($+$30.02 percentage points over plain
RankExt), while FactorOrth adds a smaller increment ($+$6.01 points on its
own and $+$5.75 points on top of KD+Protect).

The discussion below asks why the two families respond so differently to
auxiliary objectives that, at the level of the design in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, address the same problem of retention under
sequential class-incremental training, and what the open/restricted,
$R_{i,j}$, refinement, and parameter-accounting results of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} suggest about that difference. The primary
benchmark is the reference point throughout; where supporting diagnostics
from other runs or checkpoints are used, this is stated explicitly.

\section{Why SimpleAvg Is Strong}
\label{sec:ch6-simpleavg-strong}

Plain SimpleAvg reaches 75.16\% without any auxiliary mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:ch5-main-results}). This result is obtained under the
same task-agnostic evaluation as RankExt: the final merged model has a
single 100-way classifier and is evaluated with a plain argmax over all
classes, without task-identity routing at inference.

Several properties of the design are consistent with this strength, although
none is isolated as a sole cause. Each of the five rank-80 LoRA specialists
is trained from a fresh pretrained initialisation, so there is no
sequential parameter interference between specialists during specialist
training: learning a later step cannot overwrite what an earlier specialist
has learned. Classifier-row stitching (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) then
takes each class's final classifier row directly from the specialist that
trained it, rather than averaging classifier weights across steps. This
merge-then-stitch strategy sits within the broader model-merging literature
on combining independently fine-tuned weights
\parencite{wortsman2022model,ilharco2023editing}.

A natural question is whether SimpleAvg simply has more effective capacity.
Architecture-matched supporting diagnostics suggest that the merged
SimpleAvg dense update can span more effective directions than a single
rank-80 specialist or the final RankExt rank-80 adapter. These measurements
were made on related checkpoints, not on the canonical checkpoints of the
primary benchmark, and are therefore supporting diagnostic evidence only.
Two controlled capacity experiments --- a rank-32 SimpleAvg versus a
cumulative-rank-160 RankExt comparison, and a doubling of RankExt's final
cumulative rank from 80 to 160 --- closed only a small fraction of the
corresponding accuracy gap for the non-KD RankExt variants. Capacity is
unlikely to be the main source of the difference within the
tested range. The remaining performance difference is therefore examined
in Sections~\ref{sec:ch6-rankext-struggles}--\ref{sec:ch6-kd-protect} in
terms of sequential retention and output-space competition rather than
capacity alone.

One further asymmetry should be kept in view. SimpleAvg's classifier head
is trained with a learning-rate multiplier of $\times10$ relative to the
LoRA parameters, whereas RankExt's canonical configuration uses $\times1$.
Because classifier-head learning-rate parity between the two families was
not directly tested, this remains a potential confound in attributing the
full performance difference solely to integration architecture.

\section{Why KD Provides Little Net Gain for SimpleAvg}
\label{sec:ch6-kd-simpleavg}

SimpleAvg + KD reaches 75.21\% against 75.16\% for plain SimpleAvg, a
difference too small to support an improvement claim from this single-seed
experiment. The aggregate hides a redistribution: first-task accuracy
falls by 9.50 percentage points, the later-tasks mean rises by 2.44 points,
and restricted accuracy rises by 0.58 points
(Section~\ref{sec:ch5-simpleavg}).

SimpleAvg's KD objective \parencite{hinton2015distilling} distils the
logits of the previously seen classes from a frozen teacher on the current
step's training images, at temperature 2. It does not replay images from
earlier steps, and the current step's own logits are excluded from the KD
softmax. The objective therefore encourages the student to match the
teacher's relative logit structure among old classes, as it appears on
new-step images. It does not directly constrain old-class predictions on
old-step images, since those images never enter the loss, and it places no
constraint on how much probability mass old classes receive relative to the
newly introduced classes. This is consistent with the observed pattern:
the within-step restricted measure and the later-step accuracy improve
slightly, while first-step open accuracy, which depends on exactly the
old-versus-new competition this objective does not constrain, does not.

A small local gradient diagnostic confirms that the KD term is not
numerically negligible: its gradient norm is of the same order as the
cross-entropy gradient, while their cosine similarity remains close to
zero. This indicates a genuine optimisation interaction, but not strong
gradient opposition.

The KD objective is therefore not ineffective in this setting; it produces
a redistribution of final accuracy from the first-step group toward
later-step groups, with little net effect on all-seen accuracy.

\section{SimpleAvg FactorOrth: Optimization Strength versus Geometric Objective}
\label{sec:ch6-sa-factororth}

FactorOrth is a common label with a family-specific formulation: SimpleAvg
applies orthogonality to the reconstructed dense updates, whereas RankExt
applies it directly to normalised low-rank factors
(Section~\ref{sec:ch3-factororth}). This section concerns the SimpleAvg,
dense-update formulation only; RankExt's factor-space FactorOrth is
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ch6-factororth}.

Neither SimpleAvg + FactorOrth (74.02\%) nor SimpleAvg + FactorOrth + KD
(73.22\%) improves on plain SimpleAvg (75.16\%). Reducing $\lambda$ from 20
to 1 in the refinement experiment (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-ablation}) moved
the FactorOrth variant from 74.02\% to 73.80\% and the FactorOrth + KD
variant from 73.22\% to 74.64\%; neither exceeds plain SimpleAvg. Reducing
$\lambda$ from 20 to 1 produced mixed effects and does not establish
$\lambda=1$ as a superior setting.

Two separate questions follow from this result: whether $\lambda=20$
imposed too strong an optimisation pressure, and whether the dense-update
objective is aligned with the interference it is meant to control. On the
first, the evidence is limited. A one-batch local probe at the second step
found the weighted orthogonality gradient at $\lambda=20$ to be comparable
to, and after a second update larger than, the cross-entropy gradient,
whereas at $\lambda\le1$ it remained a small fraction of it. That probe,
however, applies the full coefficient from the first update, while the
canonical run ramps $\lambda$ up from zero over the first epoch of each
step, and it covers only two optimiser updates. It therefore does not
describe the gradient balance over the actual training trajectory, and the
available evidence neither establishes nor excludes excessive optimisation
pressure at $\lambda=20$ as the reason for the lack of benefit; the mixed
$\lambda=1$ result does not resolve the question either.

On the second question, SimpleAvg FactorOrth as implemented penalises each
specialist's dense update against every previous specialist's dense update,
pairwise. Because the final model is built from the average of all
updates, an alternative formulation --- orthogonality against an
accumulated historical direction, or against the joint subspace spanned by
all previous updates --- could be better aligned with the merged update
that is eventually deployed. This thesis did not test such formulations, and
does not claim that the pairwise formulation is geometrically wrong; they
are identified as future work (Section~\ref{sec:ch6-future-work}).

\section{Why Plain RankExt Struggles}
\label{sec:ch6-rankext-struggles}

Plain RankExt reaches 34.99\% all-seen accuracy against a restricted
accuracy of 91.03\%, a 56.04-percentage-point gap and the largest of any
method in the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-stability}). RankExt differs
from SimpleAvg in exactly the properties discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:ch6-simpleavg-strong}: it maintains a persistent adapter
state that is carried forward and extended across steps, its previously
learned rank blocks remain frozen but active in every later forward pass,
and a single classifier head is shared across all five steps.

The available capacity experiments do not support insufficient adapter
capacity as the dominant explanation within the tested range
(Section~\ref{sec:ch6-simpleavg-strong}). The open/restricted divergence is
more informative. The high restricted accuracy argues against a complete
loss of within-step discriminative structure, although it does not rule out
representation drift or other forms of forgetting. It is consistent with a
substantial output-space component, in which classes from different steps
compete in the shared argmax (Section~\ref{sec:ch6-open-restricted}).

Representation drift is nevertheless plausible for the non-KD
configurations. A feature-alignment diagnostic in an earlier, related run
pointed to drift of older steps' features relative to their classifier rows
in non-KD RankExt configurations. For this reason, the canonical non-KD arms
(plain and FactorOrth-only) already include a pretrained-feature anchor
(Section~\ref{sec:ch4-rankext-auxiliary}); their reported 34.99\% and
41.00\% are obtained with this mitigation in place.

\section{The Role of KD+Protect in RankExt's Recovery}
\label{sec:ch6-kd-protect}

RankExt + KD + Protect reaches 65.01\%, 30.02 percentage points above plain
RankExt. The observed recovery is attributed to the KD+Protect configuration
as a whole: full-output knowledge distillation and the projected
feature-protection term are active together, and the benchmark does not
isolate their individual contributions.

The two components act on different parts of the model. Knowledge
distillation provides an output-space constraint: in RankExt, the teacher
and student distributions span the full 100-way classifier output, which
includes the classes introduced at the current step, so the distillation
loss also constrains the relative scale of old-class and new-class logits
--- the competition that the open/restricted evidence points to, and that
SimpleAvg's old-seen KD objective (Section~\ref{sec:ch6-kd-simpleavg}) does
not constrain. The projected feature-protection term provides a
feature-space constraint: it penalises displacement of the student's
features, relative to the teacher's, along a subspace derived from the
classifier rows of the previously seen classes. Both are plausible
contributors to the recovery, and neither is established here as the
dominant cause.

\section{RankExt FactorOrth and KD+Protect}
\label{sec:ch6-factororth}

FactorOrth alone raises RankExt from 34.99\% to 41.00\% ($+$6.01 percentage
points), a much smaller recovery than KD+Protect's. Added to KD+Protect, it
raises all-seen accuracy from 65.01\% to 70.76\%. FactorOrth provides a
similar-sized additional improvement in the two comparisons: $+$6.01 points
over plain RankExt when used alone, and $+$5.75 points when added to
KD+Protect.

A plausible interpretation is complementarity. FactorOrth is a
weight-geometry constraint: it discourages the newly appended rank block
from duplicating directions already occupied by the frozen blocks, which is
a different aspect of the adapter than the output-space and feature-space
constraints of KD+Protect. The data are consistent with this reading but do
not establish synergy, and the single-seed design does not allow the size
of the interaction to be estimated. The head-learning-rate refinement
(Section~\ref{sec:ch6-stability-plasticity}) also shows that the combined
configuration responds differently from the others to a change in
optimisation settings. Used alone, FactorOrth leaves RankExt far below every
SimpleAvg variant (41.00\% against at least 73.22\%).

\section{Stability--Plasticity Trade-off}
\label{sec:ch6-stability-plasticity}

The first-task, later-tasks, BWT, and forgetting values, the $R_{i,j}$
trajectories, and the head-learning-rate refinement together describe a
stability--plasticity pattern
\parencite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming,delange2022continual}: mechanisms that
improve retention of earlier steps can reduce accuracy on later ones, and
the balance depends on how strongly and in which combination they are
applied. SimpleAvg's KD-bearing variants trade first-task accuracy for
later-task accuracy at roughly unchanged all-seen accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:ch6-kd-simpleavg}). Within RankExt, the KD+Protect-bearing
configurations substantially increase both first-step and later-step
accuracy relative to plain RankExt, whereas FactorOrth alone increases
later-step accuracy but not first-step accuracy.

The head-learning-rate refinement (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-ablation}) adds a
further observation. The effect of classifier-head learning rate is
configuration-dependent: increasing the multiplier to $\times2$ improves
three RankExt variants but changes the balance between earlier- and
later-step performance unfavourably for the combined configuration, whose
all-seen accuracy falls from 70.76\% to 67.92\%. For that configuration,
first-task accuracy rises from 60.70\% to 67.75\%, BWT improves from
$-0.1584$ to $-0.0532$, and forgetting improves from 0.2474 to 0.1289, while
the later-tasks mean falls from 73.28\% to 67.96\%. In this run, the shift
toward earlier steps outweighs the gain in retention for the configuration
with the most retention mechanisms active. Neither learning-rate setting is
therefore globally preferable for RankExt.

\section{The Local Head-Learning-Rate Probe and the Full Refinement}
\label{sec:ch6-lr-probe}

The reduced-scale probe that motivated the $\times2$ RankExt
head-learning-rate refinement tested the same combined configuration used
in the main experiment and favoured the larger multiplier within that
reduced setting (Table~\ref{tab:appA-lr-probe}). The full five-step
refinement did not reproduce this direction for the combined configuration,
although three other RankExt variants improved. This discrepancy indicates
that short, reduced-scale probes need not extrapolate reliably to a longer
continual sequence, particularly when several retention mechanisms
interact.

\section{Open versus Restricted Performance and Cross-Task Competition}
\label{sec:ch6-open-restricted}

The open/restricted divergence is the most consistent pattern across the
RankExt configurations (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-stability}). Restricted
accuracy for RankExt lies in a narrow 91.03\%--94.78\% band, while all-seen
accuracy ranges from 34.99\% to 70.76\%; plain RankExt's gap of 56.04
percentage points is the largest observed. Within-step class discrimination
therefore remains substantially stronger than task-agnostic discrimination
for the affected configurations: once competition from other steps' classes
is removed, the correct class is still usually preferred.

Several mechanisms, not mutually exclusive, are consistent with this
pattern. The observed trajectory is consistent with increasing competition
from later-step class logits against earlier-step classes in the shared
argmax: in the $R_{i,j}$ matrices (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-rankext}), each
earlier step's open accuracy declines as more steps are trained, while
just-learned and restricted accuracies remain substantially higher than
final open accuracy for earlier steps. A mismatch in score scale between
step groups is supported, for one non-primary checkpoint, by the logit
rescaling diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:ch6-calibration}. Representation
drift is plausible for the non-KD configurations
(Section~\ref{sec:ch6-rankext-struggles}). A model can retain within-step
discriminative structure, undergo some representation drift, and still
lose open accuracy through inter-step score misalignment at the same time.
The open/restricted gap is diagnostic: it does not decompose the deficit of
any configuration into causal shares attributable to these mechanisms.

\section{Inter-Step Logit Rescaling as a Diagnostic}
\label{sec:ch6-calibration}

Two different mechanisms must be kept apart here. The canonical
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} pipeline applies classifier rescaling: after
training, the classifier weight rows of each step are multiplied by a
positive step-specific factor (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-protocol}). The
diagnostic discussed in this section is a different operation:
validation-fitted, group-wise logit rescaling at inference, in the spirit of
\textcite{zhao2020maintaining}. It multiplies the logits of each step group
by one positive scalar (four free parameters, with the most recent step as
reference), fitted by minimising validation cross-entropy, without changing
the backbone, the LoRA parameters, or the classifier weights. The scalars
are fitted using the known step-group boundaries, but no per-sample step
identity is used at inference: every test image is scored with the same
rescaled classifier.

This diagnostic was evaluated on an earlier, non-primary RankExt checkpoint
that combines FactorOrth with old-seen knowledge distillation and does not
include the projected feature-protection term. On that checkpoint, raw open
accuracy was 34.67\%; the existing classifier-rescaling mechanism raised it
to 53.12\%; validation-fitted inter-step logit rescaling raised it to
70.74\%. Restricted accuracy remained 93.68\% throughout, as expected, since
multiplying all logits of one step group by the same positive scalar cannot
change the argmax within that group. The fitted scalars (2.147, 1.224,
1.178, and 0.932 for steps 1--4) decrease from the oldest to the most recent
step, meaning that older steps' logits needed the largest upward correction.

On this specific non-primary checkpoint, the large gain produced solely by
group-wise logit rescaling provides direct evidence that a substantial
component of its open-accuracy deficit was associated with inter-step
score-scale mismatch. The result is not transferred to the primary
benchmark: the checkpoint differs from the canonical configurations, and
the closeness of 70.74\% to the primary combined configuration's 70.76\% is
a coincidence of two different pipelines, not evidence that the two
mechanisms are interchangeable. Evaluating the same diagnostic on the
primary checkpoints is identified as future work
(Section~\ref{sec:ch6-future-work}).

\section{Parameter and Rank Efficiency}
\label{sec:ch6-efficiency}

RankExt's final cumulative adapter contains 2{,}949{,}120 LoRA parameters,
equal to the parameter count of one rank-80 SimpleAvg specialist. The two
methods nevertheless have different final representations: RankExt retains
a low-rank adapter, whereas SimpleAvg deploys a fused dense update
(Section~\ref{sec:ch5-efficiency}).

\textbf{Trainable-parameter accounting.} Across the sequence, SimpleAvg
optimises a fresh 2{,}949{,}120-parameter LoRA specialist at each step,
whereas RankExt introduces only 589{,}824 new trainable LoRA parameters per
step, with earlier blocks frozen. Summed over the five steps, this amounts
to 14{,}745{,}600 trained LoRA parameters for SimpleAvg and 2{,}949{,}120 for
RankExt. This establishes a difference in trainable-parameter exposure, not
a measured difference in wall-clock or FLOP cost.

\textbf{Deployment form.} In the evaluated implementation, SimpleAvg's
averaged dense update is added directly to the pretrained weights, so the
deployed model needs no adapter parameters beyond its 76{,}900-parameter
classifier, and the merged dense update is not retained as a low-rank
factorisation. If it were instead stored separately from an unmodified
base model, it would occupy 14{,}155{,}776 values, because averaging is
performed on dense matrices. RankExt's cumulative adapter is retained in
low-rank additive form alongside an unmodified base model and remains
separable from it.

The two families therefore represent different parameter-structure and
deployment trade-offs rather than a single ranking of efficiency. RankExt
adds a small trainable block at each step and keeps a separable cumulative
adapter; SimpleAvg trains a complete rank-80 specialist at each step and
deploys a fused model without a separate adapter. SimpleAvg does not
maintain a persistent cumulative low-rank adapter state across steps, which
is also why it has no $R_{i,j}$ trajectory (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-rankext}).
No wall-clock or FLOP measurements were made for either family.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:ch6-limitations}

The main limitation of this study is that the primary comparison is based
on a single seed. The reported differences should therefore be interpreted
as observed outcomes of the evaluated setting rather than as estimates of
expected performance across repeated runs. This applies to the
4.45-percentage-point gap between the two families, to the small
differences among the SimpleAvg variants, and to the head-learning-rate and
$\lambda$ refinements, which are also single-seed comparisons. The final
benchmark presented in this thesis is limited to CIFAR-100, so
cross-dataset generalisation is not established.

Some mechanisms are also not separated from one another. In RankExt, the
KD-bearing configurations always combine knowledge distillation with the
projected feature-protection term, so their individual contributions to the
largest observed recovery cannot be distinguished. Similarly, the
interaction between FactorOrth and KD+Protect is observed in two
comparisons, not isolated. With respect to the protocol, the primary
benchmark uses the $5\times20$ partition. A secondary $4\times25$
diagnostic provides one shallower-protocol comparison, but a single
alternative partition, run with a slightly different training budget and
rank increment, is not sufficient to characterise protocol-depth effects
systematically.

The continual-learning metrics have structural limits of their own.
SimpleAvg's BWT is computed against the accuracy of each independent
specialist, not against a sequentially trained model, so the BWT values of
the two families are not directly equivalent. SimpleAvg has no persistent
intermediate merged model and therefore no $R_{i,j}$ trajectory; this is an
architectural property of the method, not a missing log. The
parameter-efficiency comparison, finally, covers parameter counts and
deployment structure; wall-clock time and FLOPs were not measured, and
neither method should be regarded as computationally cheaper on the basis
of trainable-parameter counts alone.

Several interpretations in this chapter draw on supporting diagnostics:
the gradient probes, the effective-rank measurements, and the inter-step
logit-rescaling diagnostic come from supporting experiments or non-primary
checkpoints. They help explain the primary results, but they are not
primary benchmark evidence and are not used as such.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:ch6-future-work}

The next experiments follow directly from the findings and limitations
above, in order of priority.

\textbf{Multi-seed replication.} The primary benchmark and the
head-learning-rate refinement should be repeated across several seeds. The
regression of the combined RankExt configuration under the $\times2$
head learning rate is a high-priority replication target, as is the
4.45-percentage-point gap between the two families.

\textbf{Disentangling KD and projected feature protection.} Two additional
RankExt arms --- knowledge distillation without the projected
feature-protection term, and the projected feature-protection term without
distillation --- are the cleanest way to determine what each component
contributes to the largest observed recovery.

\textbf{A systematic protocol-depth study.} The $4\times25$ diagnostic is
preliminary. Additional partitions of the same 100 classes should be tested
while holding the total number of classes, the final RankExt capacity, the
backbone, the training budget, and the evaluation protocol fixed.

\textbf{An additional dataset.} Repeating the comparison on a further
visual benchmark with different class granularity or domain
characteristics would show whether the SimpleAvg--RankExt asymmetry is
specific to CIFAR-100.

\textbf{Inter-step logit rescaling on the primary checkpoints.} The
validation-fitted logit-rescaling diagnostic of
Section~\ref{sec:ch6-calibration} should be evaluated directly on the
primary benchmark checkpoints. If it remains effective there, it would be
worth studying whether it should become an integrated inference-time
component rather than an external diagnostic.

\textbf{Reformulating SimpleAvg FactorOrth.} Motivated by the absence of a
clear gain, alternatives to pairwise dense-update orthogonality could be
tested: orthogonality against an accumulated historical direction, against
the joint subspace of previous updates, or other geometries aligned with
the merged update.

\textbf{The scope of SimpleAvg distillation.} Within the rehearsal-free
setting, alternative data-free or auxiliary-data distillation designs could
constrain old-class predictions more directly than distillation on
new-step images alone. Replay-aware variants would relax the rehearsal-free
constraint and should be treated as a separate comparison.

\textbf{Comparing feature-preservation mechanisms.} The thesis already uses
two feature-space mechanisms in RankExt: a pretrained-feature anchor in the
non-KD configurations and the projected feature-protection term in the
KD-bearing ones. Comparing them directly, under matched configurations,
would clarify how much each contributes to retention.

\section{Final Conclusion}
\label{sec:ch6-conclusion}

This thesis compared two ways of integrating LoRA updates across the steps
of a rehearsal-free class-incremental sequence. SimpleAvg trains an
independent specialist at each step and averages the resulting dense
updates once, at the end; RankExt maintains a single adapter state that
grows by one frozen-and-retained rank block per step. In the evaluated
setting --- CIFAR-100, $5\times20$, a CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone, and seed 42
--- the two strategies behave very differently.

Plain SimpleAvg is a strong reference, reaching 75.16\%, and its KD variant
reaches 75.21\%; however, no auxiliary SimpleAvg variant provides a clear
all-seen improvement over the plain baseline in this single-seed
experiment. Plain RankExt reaches only 34.99\%, and within the evaluated
configurations only the KD+Protect-bearing variants approach the SimpleAvg
results, the combination of FactorOrth, knowledge distillation, and
projected feature protection reaching 70.76\%. In RankExt, these three
mechanisms act at different levels --- knowledge distillation as an
output-space constraint, the projected feature-protection term as a
feature-space constraint, and FactorOrth as a weight-geometry constraint
--- and their combination is associated with the largest recovery
observed, although their individual contributions are not isolated.

The evidence is consistent with structural differences between the two
integration strategies playing an important role in the observed
asymmetry, while the experiments do not exclude sensitivity to
optimisation choices, protocol design, or other hyperparameters. The
paired open/restricted evaluation makes one part of this picture
particularly clear. RankExt's restricted accuracy stays above 91\% even
where its open accuracy is low, so low task-agnostic accuracy cannot be
interpreted only as a loss of within-step discriminative knowledge:
competition between class groups in the shared classifier is an important
part of the observed behaviour, although it is not shown to account for the
whole gap. SimpleAvg's independent specialist training and classifier-row
stitching appear to reduce the severity of this competition in the
evaluated setting, although its final merged model is still evaluated with
the same task-agnostic all-class decision rule.

The two strategies also differ in parameter structure. SimpleAvg optimises
a complete rank-80 specialist at each step and can deploy a fused model
without a separate adapter; RankExt introduces one new low-rank block per
step and retains a separable cumulative adapter. This is a
parameter-structure and deployment trade-off, not a measured difference in
computational cost.

Overall, the experiments show that the way LoRA updates are integrated
across incremental steps matters at least as much as the auxiliary
retention objectives applied to them. The thesis contributes a controlled
comparison of two integration strategies, an analysis of stabilisation
within the persistent RankExt family, and a paired open/restricted
diagnostic view of retention. These conclusions are specific to the
evaluated setting; establishing how far they generalise requires repeated
seeds, additional datasets, and a broader range of incremental partitions.
